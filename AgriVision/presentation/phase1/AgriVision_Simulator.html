Exploratory Data Analysis

step2_eda.py — Every finding leads to a decision. This is what separates good data science from random chart-making.

Finding 1 — Yield distribution is right-skewed (skew = 1.808)
Most farms produce modest yields. A few produce enormous amounts. Like income — most people earn average, a few billionaires skew the average upward. Skew = 1.808 means a heavy right tail.

Decision taken → Use log-transform on the pesticides feature to compress the tail.
Finding 2 — THE MOST IMPORTANT CHART: Yield by crop type
This single chart caused R²=0.17 and then motivated the fix. Different crops produce completely different amounts. SVR trying to predict all crops together gets confused by the scale differences.
Decision taken → Per-crop z-score normalisation in Step 5. Converts raw yield to "how far above/below average for THIS crop?" — putting all crops on the same scale.
Finding 3 — Correlations look weak (pesticides r=0.066)
This is NOT a problem. Low correlation happens because 10 crops are mixed together. Cassava pulling yield high in one direction, Soybeans pulling it low in another, cancel each other out. Within each individual crop the climate signals are much stronger. Per-crop normalisation reveals the hidden signal.

Feature Engineering

step3_features.py — Creating smarter inputs by combining raw columns.

Label Encoding
le = LabelEncoder()
df['country_enc'] = le.fit_transform(df['country'])
# Albania→0, Brazil→12, India→42...
Computers cannot work with text. LabelEncoder assigns each unique country/crop a number. We chose this over One-Hot Encoding because OHE on 101 countries creates 111 sparse columns where every country becomes equidistant in the kernel — SVR learns nothing geographic.
Drop Year Column
df.drop(columns=['year'], inplace=True)
# Prevents temporal leakage
Yields increase every year 1990→2013 due to technology improvements. If kept, the model learns "later year = higher yield" — a time trend, not climate effects. We want climate relationships, not the calendar.

SVR Kernel Comparison

step4_svr.py — First training attempt. R² will be low — expected. We are proving which kernel wins.

Why SVR and not Linear Regression?
Climate-yield relationships are non-linear. A temperature increase helps crops in cold Norway but hurts crops in already-hot India. Linear regression draws only straight lines — it cannot capture this. SVR with an RBF kernel draws smooth flexible curves that bend to fit complex patterns.
The key structure — Pipeline prevents data leakage
python pipe = Pipeline([
  ('scaler', StandardScaler()), # normalise features first
  ('svr', SVR(kernel='rbf')) # then train SVR
])
pipe.fit(X_train, y_train) # ← THIS LINE IS TRAINING
preds = pipe.predict(X_test)
Why Pipeline? StandardScaler must only see training data. Without Pipeline you might accidentally scale the full dataset before splitting — leaking test statistics into training. Inside Pipeline = scaler fits on training data only, guaranteed.

Per-Crop Normalisation + GridSearchCV

step5_tuning.py — The key breakthrough. R² goes from 0.17 to 0.796.

The Problem — Why R² was 0.17
Cassava averages 14 t/ha. Soybeans average 1.7 t/ha. SVR sees these as completely different magnitudes. It learns which crop produces more — not what drives yield WITHIN each crop. Every per-crop R² was negative — worse than guessing the average.
The Fix — Per-crop z-score normalisation
python — THE KEY LINES crop_stats = df.groupby('crop_enc')[TARGET].agg(['mean','std'])
df = df.merge(crop_stats, on='crop_enc')
df['yield_norm'] = (df[TARGET] - df['crop_mean']) / df['crop_std']
# z-score: 0=average, +1=one std above, -1=one std below
Now Cassava and Soybeans are on the same scale. A farm producing 1 standard deviation above its crop average gets +1, regardless of which crop. SVR learns ONE universal question: which climate conditions produce above-average yields for any crop?
